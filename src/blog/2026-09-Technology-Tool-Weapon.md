"Technology is just a tool" is a phrase used to imply neutrality – an attempt to place technology outside the realm of ethical consideration. The tool, the argument goes, is neither good nor evil. It simply is.

But if we interrogate what a tool actually is, this framing starts to unravel.

A tool _empowers_ its user. It allows them to complete something they would find impossible, arduous, or time-consuming on their own. By that definition, a tool doesn't do harm – it enables its user to perform. 

A technology that does harm? We already have a word for that. We call it a **weapon**. 

The reality is that every tool is also potentially a weapon – they sit at the ends of the same continuum, often being deployed as both. Empowering its user while also inflicting harm externally. The single point of difference is that a weapon's effects land on others, not its use – which is precisely why the "just a tool" framing gets deployed so often. "I am not harmed, therefore the object is just a tool". If something can be reclassified as a tool, then it can escape all moral scrutiny. But the reclassification doesn't change what's happening. If a technology disempowers others through its use, it isn't a tool. It's a weapon.

### The Duality

An axe used in a shed or a forest becomes something else entirely if used in a school or a hospital. Not because the axe itself has changed, but because the context has. We've developed conventions and rules around tools precisely to manage this: keep certain things out of certain spaces, because the context transforms their nature. Whether we choose to acknowledge it or not, we innately understand the tool/weapon duality – we just seem incapable of applying the same logic to digital technologies. 

Social media is both a tool and a weapon. It empowers people to connect, organise, communicate, and build – while also being used to harass, surveil, radicalise, and manipulate. The technology is the same. What shifts is who is wielding it, toward whom, and in what context.

This is why acknowledging the duality over the simplicity of the binary matters. Asking "is this a tool or a weapon?" is the wrong question. The better questions are: _In this context, does it empower or disempower? Who benefits, and who bears the cost?_

A few principles worth applying to any technology:

- A tool empowers its user, not its maker.
- A technology that does harm is functioning as a weapon, regardless of its intended purpose.
- A technology that disempowers others is a weapon, even if it empowers its user.
- Context determines where on the spectrum a technology sits at any given moment. 
- A technology that consistently operates as a weapon – regardless of context – should be considered hostile and treated accordingly in all contexts. There are no "good" weapons.

Applying these principles to our AI tools, social media platforms, surveillance systems, and recommendation algorithms might give us uncomfortable answers, but they're more honest than repeating "it's just a tool."

The neutrality argument almost always comes from someone who benefits from the technology being classified as a tool. That's worth keeping in mind too.
