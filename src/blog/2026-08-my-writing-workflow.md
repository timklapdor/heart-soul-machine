One of the things that has changed over time is how I approach writing for the blog. I've tried a variety of approaches, partly because [[I don't think in words|writing doesn't come naturally to me]] – but I value what it does. Getting the ideas out of my head and somewhere else creates space and a sense of mental clarity that's genuinely good for me. That clear space allows me to move on and think about other things rather than replaying the same old loop.

At the very [beginning of my blogging](https://timklapdor.wordpress.com/) life (yes, I still need to migrate my archives across to here), most of what I wrote was essentially automatic writing. Stream of consciousness, write & hit post. The words were sometimes problematic: grammatical errors, spelling mistakes that slipped through. That editorial component of writing is something I've always struggled with. Over time, I've adapted tools to address those shortfalls because I know where my gaps are. While it would be great if this were a collaborative process, I actually enjoy being edited and having another person involved to get feedback on my writing. But I've always wanted blogging to be an independent process and not rely on anyone else, so I've used tools along the way to help fill the gap. 

One early adaptation was to speak into my phone instead of typing; then I'd edit the transcript and, through that, develop a blog post. It was faster in some ways, but I didn't enjoy the editing – and that's what I ended up spending all my time on! I started to use [Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/) to help with the editing because it does a great job of all the things beyond spellcheck that I need, and I've stuck as a paid user since[^1]. 

Then I moved to adopting an outlining process for posts, first developing a dot-point structure and coming back to fill in the details. That worked OK, and I still use it, but it lacked the organic feeling I would get from writing automatically or from speaking and transcribing.

I've come back to the transcription method, and a couple of pieces of software have really changed how I work. The is  [MacWhisper](https://www.macwhisper.com/) – a relatively inexpensive app that runs a local language model and produces remarkably good transcripts. I've tried most transcription tools as they've come out over the years, and each has had their pros and cons. MacWhisper isn't flawless, but it's pretty close and handles my accent well, manages my tendency to mumble through words. What it does a really good job of is resolving the ambiguity that comes with spoken English – where words sound similar, but only one makes sense in context. The result is still a transcript that needs editing, but the editing is much cleaner – less "what did I actually say here?" and more straightforward editorial work.

Most recently, I've added [Claude](https://claude.ai/) to the mix – not for generating posts, but for the editorial pass. There is news this week from Anthropic about adding a watermark to text generated by the tool, which is [technically quite interesting](https://declaude.org/watermarking/), but it's also caused [quite a stir](https://daringfireball.net/2026/08/anthropics_watermark_text_adulteration_in_claude_is_a_perversion_of_writing). I'm not sure if or when the tools to detect its use will arrive, but I thought I would be open about it and share my experience and how I am using the tool. 

So my workflow is pretty much the same: 
- I usually start in **[Just Press Record](https://www.openplanetsoftware.com/just-press-record/)** recording me speaking through my ideas. It saves an audio file from whichever device I'm on to my iCloud account. 
- Then I drag the audio file onto **[MacWhisper](https://www.macwhisper.com/)** and generate the transcript. 
- Then it's over to Claude where I use some basic prompts aimed at cleaning up my verbal tics and developing a structure. Typically it's something like: Here's a transcript, here's roughly what I covered and the main point I want to get across, clean up the structure, condense the parts where I ramble. It gives me back my words in a more coherent form and structure. 
- Then I copy that and head over to **[Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/)**. It's here that I read through the post and edit as I go. There are some LLM tics that I hate – I never liked 'em' dashes, however I love dash-based punctuation, but my tool of choice is the lesser-known 'en' dash. **[Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/)** is also involved, and it tends to push sentence structure to a more simplified approach – which is in keeping with my 'style' (FYI it did not like that last sentence at all!). I'm accepting and rejecting suggestions, adding and rewriting sentences and occasionally whole paragraphs. And somewhere towards the end there is a post that I'm happy with. 
- I then copy the file over to the blog site in **[Visual Studio Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/download)**, run the build script to check for errors, and if it's good, I push it to **[GitHub](https://github.com/timklapdor/heart-soul-machine)**, which builds the site and serves it on GitHub Pages and pushes shared posts to **[Mastodon](https://mastodon.social/@timklapdor)** and **[Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/timklapdor.bsky.social)**. 

There are a few steps in there and a bunch of tools. But for me, they each serve a specific purpose and role. Throughout all of this – these remain my ideas. The words, well, they've changed and evolved throughout the entire process. What I feel makes them mine is hitting that publish button and taking responsibility for them being in the world. 

For me, the role of the LLM, the AI, isn't present in the idea, and it's not in the words themselves. I assume that a watermark may say differently – but I do wonder if a pass through an LLM means that the content was in any way AI-generated. I think that distinction matters. There wasn't a prompt: write a blog post about this topic. The use of an LLM was a single step in a broader process: "Here is a blog post I've already spoken; now help me with the formatting and structure I haven't had time to do." For me, the LLM has a distinct value proposition, and it plays to what these tools are actually good at – processing text, applying pattern recognition and finding structural inconsistencies. And these are genuinely things that are not a strength of mine.

I can understand those out there who write, and many who write for a living, who find the use of LLMs abhorrent. I do find the generative use of LLMs quite problematic, and the output to be mostly just shit. But I don't feel my use falls into that same basket. The LLM is used as step in a feedback loop – it gives me something to work from, not start with. It's not a replacement for my own judgement, and I never intend to outsource that.

It's still me clicking the buttons and making decisions throughout the process. It's still my words, processed and refined yes, but still mine. 

[^1]: Somewhat reluctantly! If anyone has suggestions for cheaper or better alternatives, please let me know!!
